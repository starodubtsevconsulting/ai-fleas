# Story-driven clear-writing guide

Use this guide when an article should carry an idea through a human story rather than read like documentation, a report, or a list of correct points.

The guide draws on three compatible writing principles:

- **story creates movement**: begin with a person, situation, desire, obstacle, surprise, or change; let the idea emerge from what happens;
- **earn attention continuously**: assume the reader owes the article nothing; the title, opening, paragraphs, examples, and ending must keep giving the reader a reason to continue;
- **write plainly**: prefer simple words, concrete nouns and verbs, short sentences where they work, and remove clutter that does not carry meaning or voice.

These principles are influenced by the author's reading of *Storyworthy*, *Why Nobody Reads Your Shit*, and *On Writing Well*. Do not imitate or reproduce those books; apply the general lessons in the author's own voice.

## Shape

Prefer this movement when the material supports it:

`hook -> concrete scene/problem -> tension/question -> discovery -> wider idea -> useful consequence -> resonant ending`

Do not force every article into identical headings or a formula. The story is structural glue, not decoration.

## Required behavior

- Open as close as practical to something happening. Avoid generic throat-clearing such as "AI is changing the world" unless the sentence itself creates necessary tension.
- Give the reader a reason to care before explaining architecture, terminology, methodology, or abstractions.
- Prefer a real firsthand moment when one exists. Never invent an anecdote, dialogue, emotion, or experience.
- Let explanation arrive because the story creates a question that needs answering.
- Keep technical detail proportional to what the intended reader needs. An article for broad AI adoption should remain understandable outside software engineering.
- Use examples from ordinary work and life when they make an abstract idea concrete.
- Cut repeated conclusions, unnecessary qualifiers, inflated transitions, and paragraphs that merely restate the heading.
- Preserve humor, personality, uncertainty, and odd memorable details when they are authentic and useful.
- Make the ending do work: resolve or transform the opening question, leave a useful image/idea, or give the reader a small credible next action. Do not merely summarize every section.
- Read for rhythm. Mix short and longer sentences naturally; simplicity does not mean robotic uniformity.

## Editorial test

Before approval, ask:

1. Would the intended reader voluntarily read the first three paragraphs?
2. Is there a concrete human situation or compelling question carrying the article?
3. Does each section create enough curiosity or value to justify the next?
4. Can a non-specialist understand the central idea without decoding framework vocabulary?
5. What can be deleted without losing meaning, story, evidence, or voice?
6. Does the ending feel earned rather than appended?

If the article is accurate but reads like documentation, revise the narrative before publication.
